# Family History — Hiroshima (Mitsugi District) Roots

Goal: help Mom learn about her family background through **her grandfather** (JP's great-grandfather), who came from **Mitsugi District (御調郡), Hiroshima Prefecture**.

## What the reply tells us

- Mitsugi District no longer exists. Its land is now part of **Onomichi, Mihara, Fuchū, or Sera Town**, so we may be writing to the wrong city.
- If the ancestral *honseki* (registered domicile) is in Onomichi, they will mail us a copy of the old family register (*joseki tōhon*) for ¥750 per copy.
- We need to prove the relationship one generation at a time, using birth certificates translated into Japanese.

## Next steps

### Step 1: Find the village or town name (the blocker)

Gather everything that might name the place. Look for "Mitsugi-gun ___-mura/-son/-chō" (御調郡〇〇村/町). Also note the grandfather's **full name in kanji** and **birth date**, because the city hall will need both.

Where to look (start with what the family already has):

- [ ] **Family papers:** old letters or envelopes, photos with writing on the back, a Japanese passport, a *koseki* copy, an emigration certificate, funeral or Buddhist memorial items (*ihai* tablets, a temple name).
- [ ] **Relatives:** ask Mom's siblings and cousins, or anyone who kept the grandfather's papers.
- [ ] **Records where he settled:** immigration and passenger lists, naturalization papers, census returns, death certificate, obituary, gravestone. Many of these list a village of origin.
  - If he emigrated to **Canada**: Library and Archives Canada; the Nikkei National Museum (Burnaby, BC), which holds 1940s Japanese-Canadian registration records that often give the home village.
  - If he emigrated to the **US or Hawaii**: NARA immigration files; Hawaii State Archives.
- [ ] **Japanese-side records:** the Diplomatic Archives of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs (外務省外交史料館) has passport-issuance registers (旅券下付表) listing emigrants' honseki. The Hiroshima Prefectural Archives (広島県立文書館) and the JICA Japanese Overseas Migration Museum (Yokohama) can also help.

Once we have a candidate name, check it against the list of former Mitsugi District villages to see which **current** city it now belongs to.

> **Questions for Mom:** Where exactly did her grandfather go (country/city), and roughly when? Did she or her parent ever hold Japanese nationality or a *koseki* copy? The answers decide which archives to check first.

### Step 2: Confirm the right city hall

- [ ] Reply to Onomichi City Hall with the village/town name and ask them to confirm. Otherwise write directly to Mihara, Fuchū, or Sera Town.

### Step 3: Prepare the request (once the city is confirmed)

- [ ] **Decide who applies.** If **Mom** applies, she needs one fewer birth certificate in the chain (her own plus her parent's). If **JP** applies, the chain needs JP's birth certificate as well.
- [ ] **Build the relationship chain:** a birth certificate for each generation from the applicant up to the grandfather's child. Each one must name the parents.
- [ ] **Translate each certificate into Japanese.** Ask the city whether a self-made translation signed with the translator's name is acceptable. It usually is.
- [ ] Download the **request form** from the city's website.
- [ ] Photocopy the applicant's **ID** (passport, driver's licence, or residence card).
- [ ] Buy **postal money orders** (定額小為替) at a post office: ¥750 per copy. Buy extra, because the register may lead to more registers (see Step 4).
- [ ] Include a **stamped, self-addressed return envelope**.
- [ ] Before mailing, ask for **all** related registers for that person (除籍謄本 and 改製原戸籍謄本), not just one.

### Step 4: Read the register and keep going

- [ ] Translate the register. It lists the grandfather's parents, siblings, birthplace, and where each person moved from. This often lets us go back another 1–3 generations.
- [ ] Ask for earlier registers that this one points to, which may be in other towns.
- [ ] Optional: visit the area to look for the family temple (*bodaiji*) and graves. Temple death registers (*kakochō*) can go back further than the *koseki*.

## Caveats

- Closed registers used to be kept for only 80 years. The period was extended to 150 years in 2010, but some very old registers may already have been destroyed. Ask the city before paying for many copies.
- The *honseki* is not always where he was born. Get the exact wording right.
- Under Japanese privacy rules, registers are released only to lineal relatives (and a few others). That is why the city asks for the birth-certificate chain.
